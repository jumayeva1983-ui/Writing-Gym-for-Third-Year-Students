Look!

~3 min

A student posted this before the exam session. Then two classmates tried to rewrite its last line for a seminar paper.

“Pulling all-nighters before exams?? Don't! I tried it last term and honestly my brain just shut down. Loads of my friends say the same stuff. Sleep = better marks, guys!!”

Too casual

“Sleep = better marks, guys!!”

Overdressed

“It is incontrovertibly the case that nocturnal deprivation of somnolent repose engenders a deterioration in academic attainment.”

Seminar-ready

“Students who do not sleep before an examination may perform worse: in a faculty survey (fictional source for practice), 64 % of those who studied all night reported difficulty concentrating.”

Spot it: the seminar-ready line keeps the idea, drops the slang and the !!, uses clear academic words, claims carefully (may) and gives evidence.

!

Writing corner

~25 min

Podcast clip (from Night Owls, a fictional student podcast)

“OK, so who else crams the whole night before an exam? Me! But honestly, it's a terrible idea. I did it before my phonetics exam and my mind just went blank. Our faculty survey found loads of people do it, like most of us. And the ones who slept properly felt way better in the exam. So get some sleep, guys!”

Survey notes (faculty wellbeing survey; fictional source for practice): 210 respondents · 58 % had studied through the night before an exam at least once this year · of these, 64 % reported difficulty concentrating during the exam · respondents who slept at least seven hours before exams more often rated their preparation as effective.

7. Writing corner

Answers vary. Example (132 words): In this paper, all-night studying refers to studying through the night before an examination without sleeping. This practice appears to be common among students. In a faculty wellbeing survey of 210 students (fictional source for practice), 58 % of respondents reported that they had studied through the night before an examination at least once this year. However, all-night studying may reduce performance rather than improve it. Of the respondents who had studied all night, 64 % reported difficulty concentrating during the examination, whereas those who slept at least seven hours more often rated their preparation as effective. Since these findings are based on self-reports, they should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, they suggest that all-night studying is an ineffective strategy, and I argue that study-skills advice for students should address it directly.
