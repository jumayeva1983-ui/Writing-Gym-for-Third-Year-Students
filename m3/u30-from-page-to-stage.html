From Page to Stage

writing for conference talks

Unit 30

Today's one job: turn a written research text into a talk that people can follow by ear.

0

Look!

~3 min

A first-year student surveyed classmates about sleep before exams (fictional study for practice). Here are three ways to present the same result at a student conference.

The paper, read aloud

“In total, 31 of the 48 participants (64.6 %) stated that they had slept for less than six hours on the night preceding an examination, whereas only 7 (14.6 %) indicated eight hours or more.”

Slide karaoke

“As you can see: 31 of 48, that is 64.6 per cent. 7 of 48, 14.6 per cent. Mean 5.4 hours …” (read from the slide, back to the audience)

Spoken for the ear

“How much do we sleep before an exam? I asked 48 classmates. Nearly two out of three slept less than six hours. And a full eight hours? Only seven students. Seven.”

Spot it: the spoken version rounds the numbers, uses short sentences, asks a question, repeats the key number and talks to the audience.

!

The rule: say it for the ear

The shape of a 3-minute talk

HOOK→a question, a number, a scene→ leads to your research question
AIM→what you studied and why+ a one-sentence roadmap
POINTS→two or three findingsone key number each
MESSAGE→one sentence to remember+ invite questions

A slide that makes a point

SLIDE→headline = a sentence with the pointbody = a picture of the evidenceyou = the explanation

Build the talk

~4 min

These parts of a short talk about students' travel time (fictional study for practice) are mixed up. Choose a number (1–6): hook → aim → roadmap → point 1 → point 2 → message.

First, what they do. Most of the time, they scroll: about three quarters of all their travel minutes went to social media.
Look around you on the metro tomorrow morning. How many people are reading, and how many are scrolling?
So, if you remember one thing: short texts could turn travel time into reading time. Thank you, and I'm happy to take questions.
I wanted to know how students use their travel time, so I asked 40 students who commute to keep a one-week diary.
Second, what they wish. Half of them said they would like to read more, but only if the texts were short.
I'll talk about two things: what students do on the way, and what they wish they did.

Writing corner

~20 min

Your turn. You will give a 1-minute talk at the student conference. Write the script: a hook, your aim, one point with evidence, and a take-home message with an invitation to questions. Add at least three speaker notes: [PAUSE], [SLIDE], [EYE CONTACT]. Target: 120–160 words.

Phones in the reading room (fictional study for practice)

On five afternoons, a student observed 120 students in the faculty reading room for 30 minutes each. 84 of them (70 %) picked up their phone at least once. Students whose phone lay on the desk picked it up 6.2 times on average; students whose phone was in a bag, 1.1 times. In a short questionnaire, 45 of the 84 phone users said they had not noticed how often they checked their phone.

8. Writing corner

Model (135 words): [EYE CONTACT] How many times have you checked your phone since this session started? [PAUSE] Last month I counted phone use in our reading room, because I wanted to know how much phones interrupt our reading. I watched 120 students on five afternoons. [SLIDE 2] So what did I find? Seven out of ten picked up their phone at least once in half an hour. But the real difference was where the phone was. Students with the phone on the desk picked it up about six times in 30 minutes. With the phone in a bag: about once. [PAUSE] Once! And more than half of the phone users said they had not noticed how often they checked. [EYE CONTACT] So, if you remember one thing from this talk: put your phone in your bag. Thank you. I'd be happy to take your questions.
