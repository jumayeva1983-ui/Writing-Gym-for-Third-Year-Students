Look!

~3 min

A (fictional) study compared attendance at 8:30 and 11:00 lectures. Three students wrote its abstract. Which one could a busy reader use without the paper?

Table of contents

This paper is devoted to the problem of attendance. The data are described, and the results are discussed. Some recommendations are given.

Everything at once

As Table 2 shows, attendance at 8:30 was 61.25 % on Mondays, 63.75 % on Tuesdays … (Name, 2019). The data were entered on 12 March. This very important topic …

Informative abstract

This study compared attendance at 8:30 and 11:00 lectures in six first-year groups over ten weeks. On average, 64 % of students attended early lectures, compared with 86 % at 11:00, and the gap was largest on Mondays. The results suggest that early timetabling may reduce attendance, although other factors, such as transport, were not measured.

Spot it: the good abstract says what was done, gives the main result with numbers, says what it means and is honest about the limits. No table, no citation, no praise.

The rule: five moves, one paragraph

INTRODUCTION→Why does it matter?… is popular, but little is known about …
PURPOSE→What did the study do?This study examined …
METHOD→How? Who?past, often passive: … were interviewed
PRODUCT→What was found?past: … found, … was, … rose
CONCLUSION→So what? Limits?present + hedge: … suggest, … may

A (fictional) study of silent study hours in a dormitory. Put the five sentences in order: 1 = first.

Forty residents of one dormitory kept a study diary for three weeks before and three weeks after the silent hours were introduced.
Silent hours may be a simple way to support study in dormitories, but a longer study with a comparison group is needed.
Noise is one of the most common complaints of students who live in university dormitories.
Average study time in rooms rose from 1.6 to 2.3 hours a day, and 31 residents said that the building was quieter.
This study examined whether two daily silent study hours changed how much time residents spent studying in their rooms.

8. Writing corner

Model (125 words): Mobile phones are present in almost every lecture, but their effect on students' notes is less clear. This study compared the notes of two first-year lecture groups over four weeks. In Group A (26 students), phones were placed in a box during lectures, while Group B (24 students) kept their phones. Notes were collected after each lecture, the main ideas recorded were counted, and the students completed a short questionnaire. Group A recorded 6.4 of the 10 main ideas per lecture on average, compared with 4.9 in Group B. Eighteen students in Group A reported better concentration, although seven felt anxious without their phones. The results suggest that phone-free lectures may improve note-taking. However, the study was short, and the groups were not randomly assigned.

Keywords: note-taking, mobile phones, lectures, attention, higher education
