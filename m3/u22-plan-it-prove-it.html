Plan It, Prove It

crafting research proposals

Unit 22

Today's one job: convince your reader before you start: the study is worth doing, the method can answer the question, and you can do it.

0

Look!

~3 min

Kamola, a second-year student (fictional), wants to study English podcasts. Read three openings of her plan.

A wish

I want to research podcasts because they are very interesting and modern. I will ask everybody what they think about them.

A promise

This study will prove that podcasts improve the listening skills of all students in Uzbekistan.

A proposal

Many students listen to English podcasts, but little is known about whether regular listeners follow lectures in English more easily. This study will ask 30 second-year students about their listening habits and give them a short test based on a recorded lecture. The results are expected to show whether regular listeners score differently from other students.

Spot it: the proposal names a gap, asks a focused question, chooses a method that fits and does not promise the result.

The rule: every link must fit the next

PROBLEM→What is not known?Why does it matter?
GAP→What has research not shown yet?
QUESTION→focused · neutral · answerable
METHOD→participants · instrumentprocedure · analysis
OUTCOME→What will the answer tell us?

Three times in one proposal

KNOWN NOW→present · present perfectfew studies have examined …
THE PLAN→will + verb · will be + past participleanswers will be counted
THE HOPE→would · could · mayis expected to show whether …

8. Writing corner

Model (158 words): Many students now read their seminar articles on a phone or a laptop, but little is known about whether the medium changes how they read. This study aims to find out how second-year students read the articles set for their seminars. It will answer one question: do students who read on paper report using different reading strategies from students who read on a screen? All second-year students in two groups (about 40) will be invited to complete a 10-item questionnaire about the medium they use and their strategies, such as underlining and taking notes. Five volunteers will then be interviewed for 10 minutes each. Answers will be counted and compared between paper and screen readers, and interview comments will be grouped into themes. Participation will be voluntary and anonymous, and students may withdraw at any time. The results are expected to reveal whether paper and screen readers differ, which could help lecturers decide how to share set articles.
